This is the PSP prequel, not a port of the original Vice City - a distinction worth making before anything else. GTA Vice City Stories drops you into 1984, two years before Tommy Vercetti ever showed up, playing as Victor Vance, a soldier dishonorably discharged after a drug deal falls apart. What follows is arguably the most underrated entry in the whole series. I went in expecting a lesser spin-off riding the original's coattails. What I got was a genuinely more mechanically ambitious game, with an empire-building system and gameplay refinements that make it feel more modern than the game it's a prequel to.

Victor Vance is a different kind of protagonist than Tommy Vercetti, more grounded, more serious, still finding his footing rather than already running the show. His relationship with his brother Lance, who you'll remember as the backstabbing opportunist from the original Vice City, gives the story real emotional stakes you can see paying off years later. Victor Vance Makes This Feel Like A Different Story, Not A Retread

What surprised me most is how little this leans on nostalgia for the original Vice City. Victor isn't chasing Tommy's flash, he's a soldier trying to build something stable after the military screwed him over, and that grounding gives the narrative a different emotional register entirely. Watching his relationship with Lance develop, and understanding exactly why Lance becomes the mess he is by the time of the original game, adds retroactive weight to a character I hadn't thought much about before. There's even a genuinely memorable mission involving Phil Collins performing as himself, one of the more surprising celebrity cameos the series ever pulled off.

The Empire-Building System Is The Real Star

This is where Vice City Stories separates itself from every other PSP-era GTA. Instead of just completing missions, you're buying actual businesses across the city, nightclubs, protection rackets, drug fronts, and defending them from rival gangs looking to take them over. Each property generates ongoing income and unlocks its own side content, turning the game into something closer to a management sim layered under the usual GTA chaos. It's a clear preview of the property systems later games would build on, and it gives the mid-game a sense of forward momentum that a straight mission checklist never quite achieves.

Swimming, Crouching, And Other Overdue Fixes

The original Vice City had a well-known glaring omission: falling in water meant instant death, no swimming allowed. This prequel fixes that outright, along with adding crouching and rolling during firefights, which makes combat noticeably more tactical than the original's stand-and-shoot approach. Vehicle customization also expands, with proper upgrade options at garages for paint, wheels, and performance parts. None of these are revolutionary changes in isolation, but together they make Vice City Stories feel like a meaningfully more refined game than its predecessor, not just more of the same content.

An 80s Soundtrack That Rivals The Original

Vice City's soundtrack set an impossibly high bar, and this prequel clears it more often than you'd expect. Emotion 98.3 handles the power ballads, Flash FM covers pop hits, V-Rock brings the hard rock, and the talk radio stations remain genuinely funny even decades later. Driving down a sun-bleached coastal road with the right station playing captures exactly the atmosphere the series built its reputation on. The compressed PSP audio holds up well enough that the soundtrack never feels like the sacrifice made to fit this onto a handheld.

Multiplayer And What The PSP Version Preserves

Ad-hoc multiplayer for up to six players survives the jump to PPSSPP emulation, supporting deathmatches, team modes, and a "Protection Racket" variant that plays off the game's business system. It requires some setup to get working over local Wi-Fi through the emulator, but it's a nice bonus most players won't expect from a PSP-era GTA game. The single-player campaign remains the main draw, running 20-30 hours depending on how deep you go into the empire-building side content, but the multiplayer is a genuinely functional extra rather than a checkbox feature.

Is GTA Vice City Stories Worth Playing In 2026?

Absolutely, and if you've never played it, that's genuinely a gap worth filling. This holds up as the most mechanically ambitious PSP-era GTA game, expanding meaningfully on the original Vice City rather than just reskinning it. The empire-building system alone gives it staying power the mission-only structure of earlier games lacked, and the soundtrack and setting remain some of the series' best work. At roughly 640MB, it's a light download for the amount of content inside, and on PPSSPP with upscaled resolution, it looks better than most people remember. Don't sleep on this one.
